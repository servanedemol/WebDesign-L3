Women in history

The problem of low representation of women in the workforce is not a new problem Through this article, you will see how women participated into many inventions and sometimes did not get the full recognition for it.

Marie the Jewess (or Maria the Jewess) is a legendary alchemist from antiquity, documented by ancient writers like Zosimos of Panopolis. She developed alchemical instruments and practices. Mary's name survives in her invention of the bain-marie (Mary's bath), which limits the maximum temperature of a container and its contents to the boiling point of a separate liquid: essentially a double boiler. It is extensively used in chemical processes for which a gentle heat is necessary. This term was introduced by Arnold of Villanova in the 14th century. The term is still used for a double boiler to cook food in.

Right to educationThe right to education for women has evolved over centuries. Historically, education was predominantly reserved for men. Early societies often excluded women from formal schooling. In ancient Greece and Rome, education for women was limited to domestic skills. During the middle ages, convents provided some educational opportunities for women, mainly for religious purposes. The renaissance saw women from wealthy families receiving more formal education. In the 18th and 19th centuries, the push for women's education gained momentum. The Industrial Revolution highlighted the need for educated women in the workforce. Oberlin College in the United States was the first to admit women alongside men in 1837.

Limited carrier opportunities

  • Economic pressures: In times of high unemployment, such as the Great Depression, the practice intensified. Advocates argued that a married woman's job should be given to a man, particularly to support those with no other "breadwinner". This was reflected in federal legislation, like the 1932 Economy Act, which required the firing of one spouse if both worked for the government, disproportionately affecting women who held lower-paying jobs.
  • Married Women Ban: The 1930s would see a spike in policies and laws that discriminated against, even forbade, women to work when they were married. During the Great Depression, discrimination against their employment even became law. "Nine states had marriage [work ban] laws prior to the Depression," writes historian Megan McDonald Way, "and by 1940, 26 states restricted married women's employment in state government jobs." As women around the country struggled to make ends meet during the nation's deepest economic crisis, they became an easy scapegoat for people looking for someone to blame.
